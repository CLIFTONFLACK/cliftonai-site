Brand field guide · v3, July 2026

GetBrian

AI & automation for UK small business. Brian is the bloke who replaces the software you rent with software you own — and “brian” is one letter-swap away from “brain,” which is the whole idea. This guide covers the story, the voice, the look, the campaign, and the content to run it all.

Campaign line — “If you see Brian, get him.” Model — £1,000 build + 50% of current spend, ongoing Market — UK SMBs, initial launch
01

Brand story & strategy

Brian replaces the software small businesses rent with software they own — built fast and cheap because Brian uses AI to build it, not because the result feels like an AI product.

Where the name comes from

Brian. Flip the “ia” to “ai” and it spells brain. The joke does the positioning for you: this isn't a platform, it isn't a SaaS suite, it's the brains behind your business's software — and it's got a bloke's name because that's exactly how it should feel to deal with.

Keep that wordplay verbal. It belongs in copy, headlines and captions — not in the logo. An earlier draft rendered the wordmark as “brian” with the swap highlighted; the real mark doesn't do that, and setting it that way now reads as two competing logos. See Visual identity.

The borrowed insight

In 1986, British Gas ran “If you see Sid, tell him” to sell a privatisation nobody understood — by turning an abstract financial product into gossip about an imaginary bloke down the road. Brian borrows the same trick for AI: instead of explaining machine learning, tell people there's a fella called Brian who quietly sorts out small businesses' software.

What Brian actually does

Builds bespoke apps and workflows that replace the CRM, project management, supply-chain, and marketing/comms software a business is currently renting — with far more personalisation than any off-the-shelf subscription allows, because it's built around one business instead of ten thousand.

Who for

UK small and medium businesses, roughly 5–100 staff, typically paying anywhere from a few hundred to several thousand pounds a month spread across three to six software subscriptions that don't talk to each other.

this is the whole idea — don't over-explain it in copy, let people repeat the joke themselves

Tagline system

“If you see Brian, get him.” — primary “Half the price of your software. All of the brain.” “Sack your subscriptions.” “Brian builds it. You own it.”

Hashtags: #GetBrian   #IfYouSeeBrian   #SackYourSubscriptions

Objection handling

“Is this actually AI, or just an app?”

Both. Brian uses AI to build it fast and cheap. What you get is a normal, reliable piece of software you own.

“What if it breaks?”

Brian built it, Brian supports it. One phone number, not a ticket queue.

“What if we outgrow it?”

It's yours. Ask Brian to extend it, or take it elsewhere. No lock-in — there's no subscription to escape.

Boilerplate

25 words: Brian builds AI-powered software that replaces the CRM, project, and marketing tools UK small businesses rent — for a flat fee, then half the old bill.

50 words: Brian builds AI-powered apps and workflows that replace the CRM, project management, supply-chain, and marketing software small businesses currently rent. It's built around how the business actually works, it's owned outright, and it costs £1,000 to build plus half of whatever the old subscriptions cost — forever.

Why gold has four values

Brian Gold is sampled from the logo, and against white it measures 3.1:1. That is fine for the wordmark — WCAG exempts logotypes from contrast minimums — and fine as a fill with ink text on top, which measures 5.8:1. It is not fine for body copy, links, captions or icon labels. Those take Gold Deep. Hover states take Gold Hover, not Gold Deep, because ink on Gold Deep drops to 3.3:1. On navy grounds all of these go muddy, so reversed artwork uses Gold Light. Four values, four jobs — don't substitute one for another to “match the logo.”

Type

Space Grotesk for headings, DM Sans for body copy. Carried over from the CliftonAi era deliberately — the faces still work, and changing them would be redesign for its own sake.

The wordmark is not set in either. It has its own drawn letterforms, supplied as artwork. On the site it is reproduced as live text in Space Grotesk Semibold so it stays selectable and indexable, which is a deliberate near-match, not the real thing. Anywhere the wordmark is presentational — decks, social, print, favicons — use brian-wordmark.svg.

Logo system

The mark is a navy B whose lower counter is cut so it also reads as a G — GetBrian in one glyph — with gold circuit traces entering from the left and a gold swoosh off the tail.

Display mark — 40px and up
Compact cut — below 40px
Reversed, for dark grounds
Wordmark
Full lockup
Solo — only where traces are drawn separately

Clear space equal to the height of the gold swoosh on every side. Don't recolour the mark, don't stretch it, don't rebuild it in a typeface, and don't set the display cut below 40px.

The traces are structural — never delete them

The B has no left stem. The gold traces running into its open counter are what close the letter. Strip them and the mark reads unambiguously as a “3” — verified at 16, 24, 32 and 48px. This is the single easiest way to break the identity, and it looks like a tidy-up when you do it.

So there are three cuts, and the size decides which:

  • Display (brian-mark.svg) — 40px and up.
  • Compact (brian-mark-compact.svg) — below 40px. Same geometry with the traces thickened and the ring interiors filled, because the display cut silts into mud at favicon sizes. An optical-size cut, not a different logo.
  • Solo (brian-mark-solo.svg) — traces removed, for the one case where the surrounding artwork draws its own traces into the mark (the site's hero diagram). Never use it on its own: on its own it's the “3”.
no traces = a 3, not a B — thicken them, don't bin them

All artwork is generated

docs/GetBrian_Logo.png is the source of truth. Every SVG in public/brand/, every favicon, the OG image, the badge and the plates above are produced from it by npm run brand (scripts/trace-logo.mjs then scripts/gen-icons.mjs). Hand-edit any of those outputs and the next run silently overwrites you. Need a change? Change the source or the script.

Brian is never a face

No mascot. No illustrated character, no avatar, no stock photo standing in for him. Nobody's ever seen what Brian looks like — only what he's built. That's not a limitation, it's the mechanism: Sid was never drawn as a cartoon either, and the gap is exactly what got people talking. Represent Brian through the mark, the wordmark, and the voice — never a drawn or photographed person.

the mystery is the point — don't fill it in with a face

“Built by GetBrian” badge

A small self-contained credit for client-site footers, smaller than the client's own logo. Reads “Built by GetBrian” and links to getbrian.xyz. Copy it from public/brand/built-by-badge.html in the site repo — that file is generated, so it always carries the current mark, and it inlines both the SVG and its styles so it works on a client site with no external requests and no webfont. On a dark footer, swap the two colours noted in the file's header comment and use the reversed compact mark.

Social content & prompt library

LinkedIn carries the offer to UK SMB owners; Instagram and TikTok carry the sightings. Every prompt below assumes the voice rules from chapter 02 — and the chapter 03 rule that Brian is never drawn or photographed as a person.

30-day calendar, by theme
WeekTheme
1Teaser — “Have you seen Brian?” sightings, no explanation
2Reveal — what Brian does, the £1,000 + 50% offer explained plainly
3Proof — real case studies told as sightings
4Objections, worked-example pricing posts, direct “get Brian” CTA push

Text prompts

LinkedIn post
Write a LinkedIn post in Brian's voice: plain-spoken British, dry wit, no AI jargon (never "leverage," "seamless," "revolutionise"). Refer to Brian in the third person, like a real person, not a company. Topic: [case study / offer detail]. End with a single clear line, no hashtag stuffing — #GetBrian only. Under 150 words.
X / Twitter thread
Write a 4-tweet thread as if recounting a real job Brian did. Tweet 1 is the hook (a specific, surprising number or before/after). Tweets 2-3 explain what happened in plain terms, no jargon. Tweet 4 is the offer: £1,000 to build, half your old subscription cost after. Voice: dry, understated, British.
DM keyword auto-reply
Write a short DM reply Brian would send to someone who commented "[KEYWORD]" on a sighting post. Plain-spoken, no salesy exclamation marks, one clear next step (book a call / reply with their current monthly software spend). Under 40 words.

Image prompts

None of these ever depict Brian as a person — no figure, no silhouette, no illustrated character. The scene and the type do the work.

Wanted: Brian poster
A parody "WANTED" poster in the style of an old-west/lost-pet notice, clean white background, ink navy (#14172B) typography and rules, one Brian Gold (#BA8B32) stamp or banner. Headline: "WANTED: BRIAN." Subhead: "For building the CRM you can't afford." Typography and rules only — no photo, no illustrated figure, no silhouette anywhere on the poster.
Sighting scene, no figure
A flat-lay or empty-scene photograph implying a job just finished: an old paper invoice next to a laptop showing a clean new dashboard, on a café counter / trade-shop desk / workshop bench. No people in frame. Clean white or neutral background, natural light, ink navy (#14172B) and Brian Gold (#BA8B32) appearing only within the on-screen software, not as a filter over the photo.
Before / after bill
A clean, flat-design comparison graphic: two mock invoices side by side on a white background, ink navy (#14172B) text, Brian Navy (#0A1D3B) header bars. Left invoice labelled "BEFORE" totalling £900/month across 4 line items (CRM, project tool, marketing platform, supply-chain tool). Right invoice labelled "AFTER, WITH BRIAN" totalling £450/month, one line item. Brian Gold (#BA8B32) circle highlighting the saving.

Video prompts — faceless

Screen-capture walkthrough
Script a 30-second voiceover for a screen-recorded walkthrough comparing a client's old software stack to what Brian built. Plain-spoken British voice, dry humour, no jargon. Structure: (1) show the old, clunky multi-tab workflow, (2) show the new single-screen version, (3) state the price line: "£1,000 to build, half the old subscription after." End on the tagline.
Kinetic-text bill teardown
Script a 15-second kinetic-typography video: individual subscription line items (CRM £X, project tool £Y, marketing tool £Z) fly in and stack into a total, then get crossed out and replaced by a single smaller number in Brian Gold. No voiceover needed, on-screen text only, ink navy on white background, Space Grotesk-style bold type. No characters or figures — type and numbers only.
Animated wordmark reveal
Script a 10-second animated sting for use as an intro/outro, built from the supplied logo artwork — do not redraw or retype the mark. Beat 1: on a white ground, three gold circuit traces draw in from the left edge, terminating in hollow gold rings and solid studs. Beat 2: the navy B assembles around them so the traces land in its open counter and close the letter. Beat 3: the GetBrian wordmark fades up beneath — "Get" in Brian Navy, "Brian" in Brian Gold. Hold on the tagline "If you see Brian, get him." Flat 2D motion, no characters or figures, no letter-swap or word-morph effects.

Video prompts — presenter

Contrarian hook
Write a 30-second presenter script, contrarian-hook structure: open with a claim people expect ("everyone told this shop to buy a £200/month CRM"), then flip it ("Brian built them one for a grand, and it's theirs"). Plain British voice, direct camera address, end on the tagline "If you see Brian, get him."
Storytime
Write a 45-second presenter script in storytime structure, told as if the presenter is recounting a real job Brian did for a client, like recounting it to a mate. Specific details (town, trade, the old tool's name), one clear "and here's the number" moment, dry understatement rather than hype.
Tutorial / demo
Write a 40-second presenter script for a quick screen-record demo comparing a client's before/after stack, presenter narrating over their shoulder. Plain, matter-of-fact tone, ends with the price line and the tagline.

Pricing & offer messaging

One fixed fee to build it. Then half of whatever the client was already paying, for as long as they use it. A profit-share track exists for launch and growth work.

The build £1,000

Fixed, one-off. Brian designs, builds, and ships the replacement system. The client owns it outright.

Ongoing 50%

Half of what the client paid before, billed monthly, for as long as they use it. Never more.

Profit share by arrangement

For launch or growth work, Brian can take a share of the upside instead of, or alongside, a fee.
